Four ideas, drawn

A quantum wave hits a wall, order dissolves into chaos, one sentence gets two grammars, and a handful of possible worlds test what “necessarily” means.

Physics: a quantum wave packet meets a barrier

The picture solves the time-dependent Schrödinger equation iħ ∂ψ/∂t = −(ħ²/2m) ∂²ψ/∂x² + Vψ live. Even when the particle’s energy is below the barrier it can tunnel through, and when it is above, part of it still bounces back. Neither outcome is a classical one.

Chaos: the logistic map and its road to disorder

Iterating x → r·x(1−x) settles to one value, then splits into 2, 4, 8 … cycles, each split arriving faster by Feigenbaum’s constant δ ≈ 4.669, until chaos sets in near r ≈ 3.57. Move across the diagram to pick r and watch the cobweb on the right follow the orbit.

Linguistics: one sentence, two syntax trees

“I saw the man with the telescope” has the same words in both readings, but the phrase with the telescope attaches in a different place in the tree. That choice is what decides who holds the telescope.

Philosophy: possible worlds and what “necessarily” means

In Kripke semantics, □p (“necessarily p”) is true at a world when p holds at every world accessible from it, and ◇p (“possibly p”) when p holds at some accessible world. Click a world to flip whether p is true there. Then add conditions on accessibility and see which logic you have built.
